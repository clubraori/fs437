fs437 — five days of a living mind.

A human brain organoid, grown from stem cells in Vevey, Switzerland, sitting on a 32‑electrode array. No body. No senses. No task. Just 118 hours of neurons firing at each other, rehearsing for a world that will never arrive.

i · rhythm

Five days of firing.

Each thin vertical mark below is a one‑minute window of spontaneous activity. Higher means more spikes in that minute — summed across all 32 electrodes. The organoid is not inputless‑idle: it pulses, bursts, quiets, returns.

Look for the tall spikes. Those are network bursts — moments when thousands of neurons fire in near‑synchrony, sweeping across the tissue like a wave, then falling silent for a few seconds before the next one. In a developing fetal brain these same waves teach the cortex how to wire itself. In fs437 they have nowhere to go.

ii · voices

Thirty‑two perspectives on the same tissue.

The same five days, but now each electrode gets its own row. Darker means louder — more spikes detected at that location in that minute. Electrodes are not neurons; they are microscopic eavesdroppers listening to whichever cells happen to be nearby.

Notice how certain rows are nearly black — these electrodes sit near dense, active clusters of neurons. Others stay mostly pale. Click a row to isolate one electrode's story across the entire page.

v · shape

The shape of a firing.

Each spike has a voltage amplitude — how hard the signal pushed against the electrode. Below is the distribution, in microvolts. The two humps come from the biphasic shape of an action potential: ions rush in (the negative peak), then the membrane repolarises (the positive rebound).
